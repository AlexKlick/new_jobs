# Alex Klick



## Opening

When I built a document processing system to extract complex clinical tabular data from RTF files for biopharma regulatory submissions, I learned something that's shaped how I think about AI in healthcare: the difference between a system that works in a demo and one that clinicians can actually trust isn't about model size or clever prompts—it's about precision, reliability, and understanding the stakes of getting it wrong. That's what drew me to Pocket's mission to build an AI Front Office for rehab therapy. You're not building another generic AI assistant; you're building infrastructure that therapists and patients will rely on for critical workflow coordination.

## Role Alignment

My background aligns well with what Pocket is tackling. I've spent the last four years building RAG systems and document intelligence pipelines in regulated environments where factual correctness matters more than hype. At Credera, I designed modular agent architectures where components populate structured JSON to drive workflow automation—work that required thinking carefully about how AI systems hand off to human processes. I've operated at the intersection of technical depth and user impact, partnering with design and business analysts to translate user feedback into roadmap changes rather than building in a vacuum. The early-stage environment at Pocket appeals to me because I've seen how much ground you can cover when you can move from ambiguous requirements to shipped, observable production systems without layers of approval.

## Evidence Highlights

Two projects from my recent work illustrate what I'd bring. First, I built a RAG ingestion pipeline for a Fortune-200 biopharma client that required sub-section level PDF chunking—a technical challenge that mattered because regulatory submissions depend on retrieving the exact right passage, not an approximate match. That system now serves tens of thousands of documents to hundreds of consultants daily. Second, I designed and piloted a modular agent component model that started with ~100 users and is now scaling to 300+ with enterprise rollout planned. Both projects taught me that the hard problems in production AI aren't usually about the model—they're about retrieval quality, evaluation loops, and building systems that improve over time.

The fully remote, early-stage structure with a small team means this role would have real ownership over technical decisions and product direction—something I've found drives both better systems and more satisfying work. I'd welcome the chance to discuss how my experience with healthcare document systems and agent architecture could contribute to what you're building.
